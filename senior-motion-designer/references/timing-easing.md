# Timing and Easing

## Timing is meaning

Duration communicates weight, importance, confidence, and energy.

A 6-frame move and a 24-frame move can communicate entirely different character even with the same path.

## General temporal categories

At 30 fps, use these as starting ranges, not rigid rules:

- micro response: ~3–8 frames
- small UI transition: ~6–14 frames
- normal graphic entrance: ~10–20 frames
- major scene transformation: ~16–36 frames
- deliberate cinematic reveal: often 24+ frames

Always adapt to distance, scale, audio, readability, and tone.

## Settle time

After important information arrives, give viewers time to understand it.

Do not begin the next transition the instant a title finishes moving.

## Overlap

Overlap creates flow.

Example:
- outgoing content begins shifting,
- next scene starts forming before the first has fully disappeared,
- shared object bridges both states.

This is often superior to:
scene A disappears -> empty frame -> scene B appears.

## Ease character

Strong ease-out:
- confident arrival,
- readable finish,
- good for type and UI.

Ease-in:
- departure,
- launch,
- building momentum.

Symmetric ease:
- neutral repositioning,
- controlled mechanical movement.

Spring:
- use only when physical or playful character warrants it.

## Velocity continuity

When one object becomes another or crosses a cut, match its perceived velocity where possible.

Abrupt velocity changes make a transition feel assembled rather than continuous.

## Rhythm

Alternate intensity.

A strong sequence often contains:
fast -> hold -> medium -> impact -> calm -> acceleration -> resolve.

Constant speed becomes monotonous, even if every individual animation is polished.
